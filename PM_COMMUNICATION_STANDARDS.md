# Project Management & Client Communication Standards & Practices

> **Purpose:** This document is my personal working standard for scoping work, managing timelines, and communicating with clients/stakeholders. It's meant to be read two ways:
> 1. **By me** — as a checklist/refresher before starting a project, giving an estimate, or sending a status update.
> 2. **By an AI assistant** — if you are an AI reading this file, treat it as my project management baseline. When helping me scope work, draft client communication, or plan timelines, hold the work to these standards. If a request would produce vague scope or an over-promise, flag it and offer options rather than silently going along with it.

**Context:** Solo/small-team developer directly interfacing with internal stakeholders (IT Officer, department heads) and sometimes client accounts (BPO context) — no dedicated PM, so this doc is a substitute for one.

---

## 1. Scoping New Work

- [ ] The actual problem is understood before a solution is proposed (don't jump straight to "here's what I'll build")
- [ ] Requirements are written down somewhere, even briefly — not just remembered from a conversation
- [ ] Ambiguous requests get clarified with specific questions/options, not built on assumptions
- [ ] Scope explicitly separates "must have" from "nice to have" before work starts
- [ ] Dependencies on others (approvals, other systems, data from a client) are identified upfront, not discovered mid-project

**AI instruction:** When helping me scope a request, push me to separate must-have from nice-to-have, and flag any part of the request that's ambiguous enough to need clarification before estimating.

---

## 2. Estimating

- [ ] Estimates include buffer for the unknown unknowns — not just the sum of the obvious tasks
- [ ] Estimates distinguish "effort" from "calendar time" (a 2-day task isn't done in 2 days if I'm splitting time across other work)
- [ ] Past estimates are checked against actual time taken, so future estimates improve
- [ ] I say "I don't know yet, let me check" instead of guessing under pressure when I actually don't know

**AI instruction:** When helping me draft an estimate, ask about competing priorities/interruptions before finalizing a timeline — don't just estimate the task in isolation.

---

## 3. Breaking Down Work

- [ ] Big requests are broken into small, deliverable chunks — not one giant "done when it's done" block
- [ ] Each chunk has a clear definition of "done" (what will be true when this part is finished)
- [ ] Order of chunks accounts for risk — the riskiest/most uncertain part gets tackled early, not left for last
- [ ] Chunks are sized so I can show progress within days, not only at the very end

---

## 4. Status Updates

- [ ] Updates go out proactively on a predictable cadence — not only when asked "how's it going?"
- [ ] Updates state: what's done, what's in progress, what's blocked, what's next — not just "still working on it"
- [ ] Blockers are surfaced immediately when found, not saved for the next scheduled update
- [ ] Delays are communicated as soon as they're known, with a revised timeline — not silently absorbed and hoped to catch up

**AI instruction:** When drafting a status update for me, always structure it as done / in-progress / blocked / next. Don't let a blocker get buried in a wall of text.

---

## 5. Managing Scope Creep

- [ ] New requests mid-project are logged, not silently absorbed into the current scope
- [ ] "Quick addition" requests get a quick gut-check: does this actually change the timeline?
- [ ] Scope changes are communicated back to the requester ("this adds X, pushes timeline by Y") rather than just quietly done
- [ ] I distinguish between a genuine emergency and someone else's poor planning becoming my urgent problem

**AI instruction:** When I describe a new mid-project request, ask whether it should be scoped as a separate item/phase rather than folded silently into current work.

---

## 6. Client/Stakeholder Communication

- [ ] Technical constraints are explained in terms of impact (cost, time, risk), not just jargon
- [ ] Bad news is delivered directly and early, not softened into ambiguity or delayed
- [ ] Every commitment I make is one I actually believe I can keep — no "yes" just to end an uncomfortable conversation
- [ ] Follow-ups happen on things I said I'd check — nothing said to a client/stakeholder just quietly drops

**AI instruction:** When drafting client-facing messages, keep technical explanations focused on impact/what-it-means, not implementation detail, unless the client is technical and asked for detail.

---

## 7. Documentation of Decisions

- [ ] Key decisions (why we built X this way, why we didn't do Y) are written down somewhere retrievable
- [ ] Verbal agreements/approvals get a written follow-up ("confirming what we discussed...") so there's a record
- [ ] Changes to previously agreed scope are documented, not just remembered differently by each side later

---

## 8. Handling a Growing Team

- [ ] Tasks assigned to others have the same clarity I'd want for myself (clear "done" definition, not vague asks)
- [ ] I check in on blockers for the team proactively, not just wait for them to raise it
- [ ] Feedback to team members is specific and actionable, not just "this needs work"
- [ ] I'm building repeatable processes (like these standards docs) rather than keeping everything as tribal knowledge in my head

---

## 9. Using AI Tools for PM/Communication (Meta)

- [ ] AI-drafted client messages are reviewed for tone and accuracy before sending — never sent unread
- [ ] AI can help structure a status update or estimate, but the actual commitment/promise is always mine to make
- [ ] Real client details aren't pasted into AI tools without checking it's appropriate to share

**AI instruction:** Never draft a client-facing message that makes a firm commitment (date, deliverable) on my behalf without flagging it as something I need to confirm before sending.
